# Scoring rubric

Six dimensions, 1–5 each, weighted and normalised to 0–100.

```
score = SUMPRODUCT(values, weights) ÷ 5 × 100
```

| # | Dimension | Weight | Points moved per step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ICP Density | 25% | 5 |
| 2 | Seniority Fit | 15% | 3 |
| 3 | Competitive & Partner Presence | 15% | 3 |
| 4 | Meeting Mechanism | 25% | 5 |
| 5 | Data / List Rights | 10% | 2 |
| 6 | Prior Performance | 10% | 2 |

Tiers: **T1 Flagship ≥75 · T2 Core ≥58 · T3 Test ≥40 · T4 Decline below 40.**

The weights encode a theory: pipeline comes from being in a room full of your buyers *and* having a mechanism to actually talk to them. Everything else is secondary. That is defensible but it is not evidence. Once a client has ten or more events with real pipeline attached, re-fit the weights against what actually produced opportunities and expect them to move.

Two dimensions describe **how good the room is** (1, 2) and three describe **what access we bought** (3, 4, 5). Keep that balance in mind if you ever swap a dimension out — tilting toward access terms turns the score into "how good a deal did we negotiate," which is a different question from "should we be here."

---

## 1. ICP Density — 25%

What share of attendees are real buyers for this product.

- **5** — invite-only or hosted-buyer format where essentially every attendee fits: executive dinners, CFO networks, partner events with a controlled guest list
- **4** — a named conference for the target function; most attendees fit
- **3** — adjacent audience: right seniority, neighbouring function; or a mixed-audience event with a strong sub-track
- **2** — the target function is a minority; general business or general tech audience
- **1** — under 10% fit, or the wrong category entirely

Source it from the organiser's audience breakdown or last year's post-event report if either exists. If you are scoring from the event's name alone, say so — this is a 25% dimension and inference here moves the total 5 points per step.

## 2. Seniority Fit — 15%

Whether the people in the room can buy, champion, or neither.

- **5** — C-level and VP only, enforced by the organiser
- **4** — mixed senior: directors, heads of function, some C-level
- **3** — managers and senior practitioners
- **2** — practitioners and individual contributors
- **1** — students, academics, job-seekers

Sales tends to over-rate this and marketing under-rates it. The organiser's own delegate breakdown beats both.

## 3. Competitive & Partner Presence — 15%

Who else is paying to be in that room. This is the only dimension backed by other companies' money rather than opinion, which is what makes it worth the research time.

- **5** — several direct competitors sponsor, and/or our own partners are present; the vendor roster is focused on our category and small enough that we would stand out
- **4** — several adjacent-category vendors; the category is clearly validated; manageable vendor count
- **3** — a few software vendors among a broad mix; **or** a small invite-only event with no published sponsor list but an unambiguously correct audience
- **2** — few or no relevant vendors (services firms, banks, recruiters dominate); **or** a very large expo (200+ exhibitors) where we would be invisible even if competitors are there
- **1** — no vendors in our category at all, or the wrong category entirely

**Special cases, which come up constantly:**

- **Partner co-hosted event** — score **4–5**. There is no external sponsor list by design; the partner hosting it is the validation, and their audience is pre-qualified.
- **Client-owned event or solo webinar** — score **2**. No third-party vendor spend means no external market validation, however good the room turns out to be.
- **200+ exhibitors** — caps the score at **2** regardless of who is there. Visibility is the constraint, not validation.
- **Gated or password-protected sponsor page** — score what is visible, mark it low confidence, and recommend asking the organiser for the roster before signing. A gated roster on an expensive event is itself worth flagging.

**How to research it.** Open the event's sponsors / exhibitors / partners page. Build the client's keyword list first — direct competitors, adjacent-category vendors, and the client's own named partners — and search the page against it. Note the total exhibitor count. Record the vendor names you actually saw, not a summary; the evidence is what makes the score auditable later. Never invent sponsor names. When a whole calendar needs scoring, batch the events across parallel subagents.

Series events (an organiser running the same conference in eight cities) usually sell sponsorship globally rather than per city. Check once, apply across the series, and tell the client — it is a bundle-negotiation argument.

## 4. Meeting Mechanism — 25%

Whether we can get a scheduled conversation, or only stand near people.

- **5** — organiser pre-arranges 1:1 meetings (hosted-buyer model)
- **4** — invite-only small format where we control or co-own the guest list
- **3** — booth plus passes plus some structured networking
- **2** — booth traffic only
- **1** — no mechanism at all: broadcast webinars, large expos with no matchmaking

This is usually a factual yes/no the organiser will answer in one email. Ask before scoring.

## 5. Data / List Rights — 10%

- **5** — full attendee list before and after, with opt-in rights
- **4** — post-event list, or registration data for an owned session
- **3** — lead scanning only
- **2** — nothing beyond what we collect ourselves
- **1** — explicit contractual prohibition on contacting attendees

Read the contract, not the prospectus. This is also the most negotiable item on the list — a 3 can often be bought up to a 5 for nothing.

## 6. Prior Performance — 10%

- **5** — ran it, beat target
- **4** — ran it, roughly hit target
- **3** — never run, or ran it with no measurement — **the honest default**
- **2** — ran it, underperformed
- **1** — ran it, clearly failed

Until the client has debrief data, this will be 3 almost everywhere and it contributes a flat +6 to every score, discriminating nothing. That is fine — it is a placeholder that becomes valuable in year two. Do not fabricate variation here to make the model look busier.

---

## Reporting a score honestly

Always state which dimensions came from the client's own data and which came from your inference. A useful shape:

> Score 67 (T2 Core). Meeting Mechanism and Data Rights read from your sponsorship terms; ICP Density and Seniority inferred from the event's positioning; Prior Performance is the unknown default.

**Flag scores within 4 points of a tier boundary.** One judgement call flips them, and a tier label carries more weight in a meeting than the number does.

**Watch for clustering.** If several events end up with identical scores, that is a signal you scored a category rather than the individual events. Real events differ. Go back and separate them.
