# Payback model

## The chain

```
required ARR        = all-in cost ÷ (gross margin × payback years)
required pipeline   = required ARR ÷ win rate
required wins       = required ARR ÷ average contract value
required opps       = required wins ÷ win rate
meeting target      = required opps ÷ meetings-to-opportunity rate      (round up)

break-even cost per won customer = ACV × margin × payback
required close rate = cost per ICP prospect ÷ break-even cost per won customer
required pipe:spend = 1 ÷ (margin × payback × win rate)
```

Note that **required pipe:spend is independent of cost**. It is a property of the client's economics, not of any event. That makes it a single sentence worth putting in front of leadership: *every dollar of event spend has to return N dollars of pipeline.*

## Worked example

$41,000 all-in · 50% margin · 1.5 year payback · 15% win rate · $80,000 ACV · 30% meetings-to-opp

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Required ARR | 41,000 ÷ (0.50 × 1.5) | $54,667 |
| Required pipeline | 54,667 ÷ 0.15 | $364,444 |
| Required wins | 54,667 ÷ 80,000 | 0.68 customers |
| Required opportunities | 0.68 ÷ 0.15 | 4.6 → **5** |
| Meeting target | 4.6 ÷ 0.30 | 15.2 → **16** |
| Break-even per won customer | 80,000 × 0.5 × 1.5 | $60,000 |

The meeting target is the output that changes behaviour, because it is an instruction rather than a metric.

## The win-rate substitution

The most valuable single move in this whole skill: **use the actual trailing win rate, not the planning one.** Companies routinely model events against an aspirational rate. Substituting the real one typically doubles the required pipeline.

Show both. A table with the aspirational and actual side by side lets leadership choose — and choosing the aspirational number becomes a visible decision rather than a default.

## The rounding trap

Per-event figures round up: even a $500 webinar demands at least one opportunity and a handful of meetings. Across a 30-event portfolio that inflates the totals badly — summed rounded opportunities can run 20–25% above the true program requirement.

**Quote per-event numbers rounded up** (they are instructions and must be whole). **Quote portfolio numbers from unrounded program-level maths** (they are targets). Say which you are doing. Getting caught quoting an inflated portfolio total undermines everything else in the model.

## Sensitivity

The two inputs that move the answer most are win rate and payback period, and both are usually contested.

- Halving the win rate doubles required pipeline.
- Going from an 18-month to a 12-month payback target raises the bar by 50%.
- Margin and ACV matter less than people expect, because they appear on both sides of related ratios.

Run the model at the client's stated inputs, then at one plausible pessimistic set, and show the gap. If the decision flips between them, the decision is really about the assumption and should be escalated as such.

## The four questions finance always asks

**"Why that win rate?"** Because it is what the last twelve months actually produced. Show the aspirational number alongside and let them pick — but make the pick explicit.

**"Is that the real cost?"** Sponsorship fee is rarely the whole number. Travel, swag, print, shipping and staff time typically add 15–30%. If those are estimates rather than tracked actuals, say so before someone finds out.

**"Sourced or influenced?"** This must be defined before any number is quoted, and it changes the result substantially. Sourced is stricter and more defensible; influenced is larger and easier to dispute. Bring it as a decision to be made rather than an assumption you already made.

**"What did last year's events actually produce?"** If the answer is "we don't know," that is the finding — and it is more important than any individual event decision. A program that cannot answer this cannot defend its budget, regardless of how good the events are.

## Using it to set a spend ceiling

Run the chain backwards to find the most an event can be worth:

```
max defensible spend = (expected ICP prospects × plausible close rate) × ACV × margin × payback
```

At 50 qualified prospects, a 1% close rate, $80,000 ACV, 50% margin and 1.5 years: $30,000. If the organiser is asking $45,000, the gap is the negotiation — or the decline.

This is a better opening position than reacting to their price, and it gives the client a number to hold rather than a feeling.
